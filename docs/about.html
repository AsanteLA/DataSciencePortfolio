About

Hi, I'm Asante.

I'm a data-minded student building my way into analytics work where the modeling is as rigorous as the storytelling. This project is one of the ones I'm happiest with.

How I worked with AI on this project

I used Claude as a pair programmer through the whole project — aggressively for knowledge transfer on the modeltime package, conservatively for code generation. I'd never touched modeltime, Prophet, or ARIMA before this week, and the fastest path to fluency was a continuous planning chat where I could ask real questions and push back when a suggestion didn't hold up.

Knowledge transfer

Compress the learning curve

First hour was spent asking Claude to map the tidymodels workflow I already knew onto the modeltime pipeline, explain calibration, and teach me why time series can't be split randomly. The transcript is in PLANNING_CHAT.md.

Code I wrote myself

Own the important lines

Every recipe, workflow, model spec, and the custom future-frame logic. I wanted to be able to defend every line in a code review.

Code I delegated

Boilerplate is Claude's job

Glue code like future_frame() + ISO-week seasonal-naive lookups. I read every draft carefully, rewrote the coalesce() safety net after spotting the week-52/53 edge case, and moved on.

Where I pushed back

Claude was wrong about MAPE

Claude suggested selecting on MAPE because "ops reasons in percentages." I argued the overtime budget thinks in absolute units, not percentages — so the selection metric had to match the cost structure. RMSE for selection, MAPE for reporting. That disagreement ended up sharpening the whole spec.

The short version

I wanted AI to compress my learning curve on modeltime, not outsource my judgment. Ownership of modeling decisions and interpretation of results had to stay with me. I think that's the bar for AI-native engineering work — and it's the bar I'm calibrating toward as I move into professional data roles.

What I'm taking forward

  1. Small series reward discipline, not cleverness. With 131 rows, the best model was the simplest of the three. I spent real energy resisting the urge to tune hyperparameters harder, and I think that discipline will pay off.
  2. The overlay plot tells a story metrics can't. Pure ARIMA's RMSE is just a number. The plot shows exactly where and how it failed. That's what an exec actually cares about.
  3. Prompting AI well is its own skill. The best moments in my planning chat were when I pushed back on a confident suggestion. Good prompts look less like "write me X" and more like "here's my thinking; critique it."
